\begin{abstract}
This note explains what \texttt{pam\_st} computes when run with
\texttt{--statistic minp}, the significance search that reports the motifs
least explained by chance, for one tissue or for several analysed together.
Every idea is given three times: in plain words, as mathematics, and as a small
worked example with exact numbers. The plain-language boxes can be read on
their own; the definitions and propositions state precisely what the code
implements and under which assumptions its $p$-values are valid.
Section~\ref{sec:evidence} reports the simulations that check those
guarantees, and Section~\ref{sec:real} a run on four PDAC tissues. Every
symbol is defined where it first appears; Section~\ref{sec:notation} gives the
general notation and Appendix~\ref{app:symbols} lists every symbol with the
place that defines it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Notation}
\label{sec:notation}

\begin{plain}
Maths is a short-hand. Each letter that stands for something in this pipeline
--- a cell, a count, a number of shuffles --- is explained where it first
appears. This section collects the general-purpose symbols that the rest of
the note uses without further comment.
\end{plain}

\begin{description}[style=unboxed,leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=3pt]
  \item[Sets.] $\{y:\text{condition}\}$ is the set of all $y$ satisfying the
    condition. $|A|$ is the number of elements of a finite set $A$;
    $A\cap A'$, $A\cup A'$ and $A\subseteq A'$ are intersection, union and
    inclusion; $\emptyset$ is the empty set; $\bigsqcup_s A_s$ is a union of
    sets that do not overlap. A \emph{multiset} is a collection in which
    repetitions count, written $\mset{\dots}$; a \emph{partition} of $A$ is a
    collection of non-empty, non-overlapping subsets whose union is $A$.
  \item[Numbers.] $\Real$ is the real numbers and $\Real^2$ the plane;
    $\Int$ is the integers and $\Nat=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$. $\Nat^K$ is the set of
    vectors of $K$ non-negative integers; for such a vector $h$, $h[t]$ is its
    $t$-th entry. $\lfloor y\rfloor$ is the largest integer not above $y$, and
    $y\bmod 2$ the remainder of the integer $y$ after division by 2.
    $\gcd(h)$ is the greatest common divisor of the entries of $h$.
    For integers $0\le w\le m$, $m!=1\cdot2\cdots m$ (with $0!=1$), and
    $\binom{m}{w}=m!/(w!\,(m-w)!)$ is the number of ways to choose $w$ of $m$
    items. $\ln$ is the natural logarithm, with the
    convention $0\ln0=0$; $\arcsin$ is the inverse sine.
  \item[Length.] For $y=(y_1,\dots,y_K)\in\Real^K$,
    $\lVert y\rVert_2=\sqrt{y_1^2+\dots+y_K^2}$ is its Euclidean length.
  \item[Sums and extremes.] $\sum$ and $\prod$ are sums and products;
    $\min_{j\ge k}$ and $\max_{j\le k}$ are the smallest and largest value
    over the indices shown.
  \item[Restriction.] For a labelling $\lab$ (Definition~\ref{def:tissue})
    and a set of cells $A$, $\lab|_A$ is the list of labels of the cells in
    $A$ only.
  \item[Permutations.] $\Sym(A)$ is the set of permutations of a finite set
    $A$, i.e.\ one-to-one maps $\psi$ of $A$ onto itself. A permutation $\psi$
    of a set of cells moves labels: $(\psi\lab)_{\psi(i)}=\lab_i$, and cells
    outside the set keep their label. For non-overlapping sets
    $A_1,\dots,A_G$, $\prod_g\Sym(A_g)$ is the set of permutations that map
    each $A_g$ onto itself.
  \item[Probability.] $\Pr(E)$ is the probability of the event $E$ under the
    null model being discussed. \emph{i.i.d.}\ means independent and
    identically distributed. Random quantities $Y_1,\dots,Y_m$ are
    \emph{exchangeable} when their joint distribution does not change if
    their indices are permuted.
  \item[Approximation and cost.] $\approx$ is ``approximately equal'' and
    $\gtrsim$ ``at least approximately''. $O(f)$ is a cost at most a constant
    times $f$.
  \item[Logic.] $\exists$ is ``there exists''; $y:=\dots$ defines $y$.
\end{description}

\section{The picture: cells, circles and recipes}
\label{sec:data}

\begin{plain}
Think of a tissue as a huge map covered in marbles glued in place. Each marble
is a cell and its colour is the cell type. Around every marble we draw a small
circle and write down what is inside: say \emph{5 red and 3 blue}. That list is
a \emph{recipe}. We care about the mix, not the size: \emph{5 red, 3 blue} and
\emph{10 red, 6 blue} are the same recipe. Two recipes count as matching when
their mixes are almost the same. A \emph{motif} is a recipe that keeps coming
back.
\end{plain}

\begin{definition}[Tissue]
\label{def:tissue}
A tissue is a finite set of $n$ cells $\cells=\{1,\dots,n\}$. Cell $i$ has a
position $x_i=(x_{i,1},x_{i,2})\in\Real^2$ and a label $\lab_i\in\types$,
where $\types=\{1,\dots,K\}$ numbers the $K$ cell types. A \emph{labelling} is
the list $\lab=(\lab_1,\dots,\lab_n)$; the one read from the data is the
\emph{observed labelling} $\lab^{\obs}$. Cells annotated
\texttt{unclassified} are removed before analysis. Positions never change;
the null models of Section~\ref{sec:null} produce other labellings of the
same positions.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Neighbourhood and composition]
\label{def:nbhd}
For a radius $r>0$, the neighbourhood of cell $i$ is
$\Nb{i}=\{j\in\cells:\lVert x_j-x_i\rVert_2\le r\}$, which contains $i$
itself. Its \emph{composition} under a labelling $\lab$ is the count vector
$h_i(\lab)\in\Nat^K$ with $h_i(\lab)[t]=|\{j\in \Nb{i}:\lab_j=t\}|$ for
$t\in\types$. A cell used this way is called a \emph{centre}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Matching]
\label{def:match}
Let $h,h'\in\Nat^K$ be count vectors that are not all zero.
With \texttt{--metric l2}, write $u(h)=h/\lVert h\rVert_2$ and
$d(h,h')=\lVert u(h)-u(h')\rVert_2\in[0,\sqrt2]$, the chord distance between
the two directions. With \texttt{--metric js}, write
$\phi(h)=h/\sum_{t=1}^K h[t]$ for the proportions,
$\operatorname{Ent}(\phi)=-\sum_{t=1}^K\phi[t]\ln\phi[t]$ for their entropy, and
\[
  \JS(\phi,\phi')=\operatorname{Ent}\bigl(\tfrac12(\phi+\phi')\bigr)-\tfrac12\bigl(\operatorname{Ent}(\phi)+\operatorname{Ent}(\phi')\bigr)\in[0,\ln2],
  \qquad
  d(h,h')=\sqrt{\JS\bigl(\phi(h),\phi(h')\bigr)},
\]
the square root of the Jensen--Shannon divergence. For a tolerance
$\rho\ge0$, $h$ and $h'$ \emph{match} when $d(h,h')\le\rho$; for $\rho=0$
this is exact proportional equality.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Composition classes]
\label{def:classes}
Two count vectors are \emph{equivalent} when they are proportional, i.e.\
$h/\gcd(h)=h'/\gcd(h')$; then $d(h,h')=0$ under both metrics, so any third
vector matches both or neither. For a set of centres $S\subseteq\cells$ and a
labelling $\lab$, the \emph{classes} of $S$ are the groups of equivalent
vectors among $\{h_i(\lab):i\in S\}$, numbered $q=1,\dots,Q$ in order of first
appearance (smallest centre index). The \emph{representative} $\hat h_q$ of
class $q$ is $h_i(\lab)$ for the smallest centre $i\in S$ in that class.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Frequency and disjoint support]
\label{def:freq}
For a \emph{query vector} $v\in\Nat^K$ (not all zero), a set of centres
$S\subseteq\cells$ and a labelling $\lab$, the \emph{frequency} is
$N(v;\lab,S)=|\{i\in S:d(h_i(\lab),v)\le\rho\}|$. The \emph{disjoint support}
$D(v;\lab,S)$ is found by going through the matching centres of $S$ in
increasing index order and keeping a centre when its neighbourhood shares no
cell with the neighbourhoods of the centres already kept; $D$ is the number
kept.
\end{definition}

Frequency counts overlapping circles separately, so one dense clump counts many
times; disjoint support counts it once. Because $d(h_i(\lab),v)$ depends only
on the class of $h_i(\lab)$, the implementation answers one query per class,
with a KD-tree for the chord distance and a vantage-point tree for
Jensen--Shannon, which gives the same $N$.

\begin{example}{how close is ``almost the same''?}
With two types, counts written as (red, blue), and \texttt{--rho 0.05}:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Recipe & Chord distance $d$ to $(5,3)$ & Match? \\
\midrule
$(10,6)$ & $0$ (same mix, twice the size) & yes \\
$(6,3)$ & $0.077$ & no \\
$(5,4)$ & $0.134$ & no \\
$(4,4)$ & $0.244$ & no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Two directions at an angle $\theta$ have chord $2\sin(\theta/2)$, so
$\rho=0.05$ allows an angle of $2\arcsin(0.025)=2.87^\circ$: a tight match.
It also explains an old artefact. The recipe \emph{20 fibroblasts, 1 immune
cell} sits at distance $0.04995$ from \emph{pure fibroblasts}, just inside the
tolerance, so it matches every pure-fibroblast neighbourhood --- which is why
the original frequency test kept reporting
\texttt{Other\_fibroblasts:20; Other\_immune:1}.
\end{example}

\section{The luck machine: null models}
\label{sec:null}

\begin{plain}
Some recipes come back again and again. Is that because cells really like to
sit that way, or just luck? To find out we build pretend maps: every marble
stays where it is, but we peel off the colour stickers and stick them back on
at random. There is one rule. Some parts of a tissue naturally have more of
one colour, like a forest has more trees than a beach. So we cut the map into
squares and only swap stickers \emph{inside} each square. Every square keeps
exactly its own number of each colour; only the fine arrangement gets mixed
up.
\end{plain}

\begin{definition}[Block null]
\label{def:block}
Fix a tile side $L>0$ and a grid origin $o=(o_1,o_2)\in\Real^2$. The tile
index of a point $\xi=(\xi_1,\xi_2)\in\Real^2$ is
$\gamma_L(\xi)=\bigl(\lfloor (\xi_1-o_1)/L\rfloor,\lfloor (\xi_2-o_2)/L\rfloor\bigr)\in\Int^2$.
Cells whose positions share a tile index form a \emph{tile}; the $G$ non-empty
tiles $W_1,\dots,W_G$ partition $\cells$, and $\tiles=\{W_1,\dots,W_G\}$. Let
$\Pi_{\tiles}=\prod_{g=1}^G\Sym(W_g)$, the permutations that keep every cell
in its tile. The \emph{block null hypothesis} $H_0^{\mathrm{blk}}$ states
that, conditional on the positions and on the label multiset
$\mset{\lab^{\obs}_i:i\in W_g}$ of every tile, $\lab^{\obs}$ is uniformly
distributed over $\{\psi\lab^{\obs}:\psi\in\Pi_{\tiles}\}$. The global null is
the case $G=1$: a single tile holding the whole tissue.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[How the pretend maps are drawn]
\label{rem:draws}
Let $B$ be the number of permutation draws (\texttt{--permutations}). The
code sets $\lab^{(0)}=\lab^{\obs}$ and $\lab^{(b)}=\psi_b\lab^{(b-1)}$ for
$b=1,\dots,B$, with $\psi_1,\dots,\psi_B$ independent and uniform on
$\Pi_{\tiles}$. The product $\psi_b\cdots\psi_1$ is again uniform on
$\Pi_{\tiles}$ and independent of $\psi_{b-1}\cdots\psi_1$, so the draws
$\lab^{(1)},\dots,\lab^{(B)}$ are i.i.d.\ and uniform over the rearrangements
allowed by the null.
\end{remark}

\section{How surprising? The \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-value}
\label{sec:p}

\begin{plain}
We make many pretend maps --- thousands --- and count the recipe on each. Then
we ask: out of all those maps, how many had the recipe at least as often as the
real one? If hardly any did, luck is a poor explanation. The key idea: if
nothing special were going on, the real map would just be one more pretend map,
equally likely to land anywhere in the line-up. Landing at the very top by
chance happens only one time in $B+1$.
\end{plain}

\begin{definition}[Pooled-rank $p$-value]
\label{def:p}
Fix a query vector $v$ and a set of centres $S$. Let
$N^{(b)}=N(v;\lab^{(b)},S)$ for $b=0,1,\dots,B$, and write
$N^{\obs}=N^{(0)}$ for the observed frequency. For a number $y$, let
$R(y)=|\{b\in\{0,\dots,B\}:N^{(b)}\ge y\}|$ count how many of the $B+1$
values, repetitions included, are at least $y$. Then
\[
  p=\frac{R(N^{\obs})}{B+1}=\frac{1+|\{b\in\{1,\dots,B\}:N^{(b)}\ge N^{\obs}\}|}{B+1},
  \qquad
  p^{(b)}=\frac{R(N^{(b)})}{B+1}\quad(b=1,\dots,B).
\]
$p$ is the $p$-value of the observation; $p^{(b)}$ scores draw $b$ in the same
way and is used in Section~\ref{sec:wy}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Effect sizes]
\label{def:effect}
With the notation of Definition~\ref{def:p}, the null mean and standard
deviation are $\bar N=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B N^{(b)}$ and
$\hat s=\bigl(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B(N^{(b)}-\bar N)^2\bigr)^{1/2}$. The
\emph{lift} is $\lift=N^{\obs}/\bar N$, set to $\infty$ when
$\bar N=0<N^{\obs}$ and to $1$ when both are $0$. The \emph{$z$-score} is
$z=(N^{\obs}-\bar N)/\hat s$, set to $\pm\infty$ when $\hat s=0$ and
$N^{\obs}\ne\bar N$, and to $0$ when $\hat s=0$ and $N^{\obs}=\bar N$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Validity]
\label{prop:valid}
If $N^{(0)},N^{(1)},\dots,N^{(B)}$ are exchangeable, then
$\Pr(p\le\alpha)\le\alpha$ for every level $\alpha\in(0,1)$, with equality at
multiples of $1/(B+1)$ when ties have probability zero. Under
$H_0^{\mathrm{blk}}$ they are exchangeable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under $H_0^{\mathrm{blk}}$, $\lab^{\obs}$ is uniform over the allowed
rearrangements and, by Remark~\ref{rem:draws}, so is each draw, independently;
the $B+1$ labellings are therefore i.i.d.\ and the counts exchangeable.
Exchangeability makes the rank of $N^{\obs}$ among the $B+1$ values uniform
(ties only make $R$ larger), so $\Pr\bigl(R(N^{\obs})\le k\bigr)\le k/(B+1)$ for
every integer $k=1,\dots,B+1$; take $k=\lfloor\alpha(B+1)\rfloor$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}{nine pretend maps}
A recipe appears 12 times on the real map. On $B=9$ pretend maps it appears
$4,6,3,5,7,2,4,6,5$ times. None reaches 12, so $R(12)=1$ and
$p=1/10=0.1$. That is the smallest $p$ nine maps can ever give: to show that
something happens less than once in a thousand by chance you need thousands of
pretend maps (Section~\ref{sec:budget}). Here $\bar N=42/9\approx4.7$, so the
lift is $12/4.7\approx2.6$.
\end{example}

\section{Trap 1: drawing the target after shooting the arrow}
\label{sec:split}

\begin{plain}
If you pick a recipe \emph{because} you saw it a lot, it will of course look
common --- like shooting an arrow and then drawing the target around it. To
avoid this we colour the map like a chessboard. The grey squares are used to
\emph{choose} which recipes to test, and the white squares only to
\emph{check} them. A circle is used only if it fits entirely inside its own
square, so a checking circle can never share a marble with a choosing one. And
the pretend maps only shuffle stickers on the white squares: the grey squares
stay exactly as they were when the recipes were chosen.
\end{plain}

\begin{definition}[Checkerboard split]
\label{def:split}
Fix a split tile side $\Lambda>2r$ (\texttt{--split-size}). The split grid has
the origin $o$ of Definition~\ref{def:block} under the block null and origin
$(0,0)$ under the global null. For cell $i$ let
$(a_{i,1},a_{i,2})=\gamma_\Lambda(x_i)$ be its split tile index. Cell $i$ is
\emph{interior} when its circle fits inside its split tile:
\[
  o_e+a_{i,e}\Lambda+r\ \le\ x_{i,e}\ \le\ o_e+(a_{i,e}+1)\Lambda-r
  \qquad\text{for both coordinates } e=1,2.
\]
Its \emph{parity} is $\pi(i)=(a_{i,1}+a_{i,2})\bmod 2$. The selection centres
are $S_0=\{i\text{ interior}:\pi(i)=0\}$ and the testing centres
$S_1=\{i\text{ interior}:\pi(i)=1\}$. The selection half
$A_0=\{i\in\cells:\pi(i)=0\}$ and the testing half
$A_1=\{i\in\cells:\pi(i)=1\}$ contain all cells, interior or not.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Separation]
\label{lem:sep}
For $i\in S_1$, $\Nb{i}\subseteq A_1$; for $j\in S_0$, $\Nb{j}\subseteq A_0$;
hence $\Nb{i}\cap\Nb{j}=\emptyset$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Interiority puts every point within distance $r$ of $x_i$ inside the closed
square of $x_i$'s split tile. The inequalities are not strict, so containment
can fail only for a neighbour lying exactly on the upper edge of the tile at
distance exactly $r$, a probability-zero event for measured coordinates.
Tiles of different parity do not overlap.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Candidate family]
\label{def:family}
Fix a support floor $\kappa$ (\texttt{--min-support}) and an optional
composition filter: at least $\mtypes$ cell types (\texttt{--min-types}), each
with at least $\mcells$ cells (\texttt{--min-type-cells}). Take the classes of
$S_0$ under $\lab^{\obs}$ (Definition~\ref{def:classes}), with
representatives $\hat h_1,\dots,\hat h_Q$. The candidate family is
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal F=\bigl\{\hat h_q:\ &N(\hat h_q;\lab^{\obs},S_0)\ge \kappa,\
  D(\hat h_q;\lab^{\obs},S_0)\ge \kappa,\
  |\{t\in\types:\hat h_q[t]\ge \mcells\}|\ge \mtypes\bigr\}.
\end{align*}
With $\mtypes=0$ the last condition always holds. $C=|\mathcal F|$ is the
family size, and $v_1,\dots,v_C$ are its members in increasing order of $q$;
candidate $c\in\{1,\dots,C\}$ is the query vector $v_c$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Measurability]
\label{lem:meas}
$\mathcal F$ is a function of $\lab^{\obs}|_{A_0}$ alone.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\mathcal F$ uses only the compositions $h_i(\lab^{\obs})$ and match sets of
centres $i\in S_0$, whose neighbourhoods lie in $A_0$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Conditional null]
\label{def:condnull}
$H_0^{\mathrm{blk},1}$ states that, conditional on the positions, on
$\lab^{\obs}|_{A_0}$ and on the label multiset of every $W_g\cap A_1$,
$\lab^{\obs}$ is uniformly distributed over
$\{\psi\lab^{\obs}:\psi\in\prod_{g=1}^G\Sym(W_g\cap A_1)\}$: labels move only
within the testing part of each tile. The draws $\lab^{(b)}$ are generated as
in Remark~\ref{rem:draws} with this set of permutations.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}{what happened before the split existed}
The first version chose the family and tested it on the same neighbourhoods.
On tissues with uniformly placed cells and independently drawn labels ---
where by construction there is nothing to find --- every dataset that produced
a candidate declared it significant: 5 out of 5. The reason: a recipe that
reached support 10 by chance was chosen \emph{because} it did, while its
pretend-map counts, which never chose it, sat near zero. After the split, none
of 40 such datasets reported anything.
\end{example}

\section{Trap 2: buying hundreds of lottery tickets}
\label{sec:wy}

\begin{plain}
We do not test one recipe but hundreds. If you buy 823 lottery tickets, one of
them probably wins by luck. So we ask a tougher question. On every pretend map
we look at the \emph{luckiest} of all the recipes, and a real recipe only
counts if it beats the best that luck could offer anywhere in the list. Then we
repeat without the winner, to judge the second best against the luckiest of
the rest, and so on.
\end{plain}

For each candidate $c$, apply Definitions~\ref{def:p} and~\ref{def:effect}
with $v=v_c$ and $S=S_1$. This gives the counts $N^{\obs}_c$ and $N^{(b)}_c$,
the $p$-values $p_c$ and $p^{(b)}_c$, the null mean $\bar N_c$, the lift
$\lift_c$ and the $z$-score $z_c$.

\begin{definition}[Candidate hypotheses]
\label{def:hyp}
The hypothesis $H_c$ states that $N^{\obs}_c,N^{(1)}_c,\dots,N^{(B)}_c$ are
exchangeable. Candidate $c$ is a \emph{true null} when $H_c$ holds; $I_0$ is
the set of true nulls. $H_0^{\mathrm{blk},1}$ implies every $H_c$ at once (the
\emph{complete null}). \emph{Subset pivotality} is the assumption that the
joint distribution of $(p_c)_{c\in I_0}$ is the same as under the complete
null, whichever other hypotheses are false.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Westfall--Young step-down on the minimum $p$-value]
\label{def:wy}
Rank the candidates by increasing $p_c$, breaking ties by decreasing
$\lift_c$ and then by increasing $c$. Write $(1),\dots,(C)$ for the candidates
in this order, so that $p_{(1)}\le\dots\le p_{(C)}$. For $k=1,\dots,C$ and
$b=1,\dots,B$,
\[
  \minp^{(b)}_k=\min_{j\ge k}p^{(b)}_{(j)},\qquad
  \tilde p_{(k)}=\frac{1+|\{b:\minp^{(b)}_k\le p_{(k)}\}|}{B+1},\qquad
  \bar p_{(k)}=\max_{j\le k}\tilde p_{(j)}.
\]
$\minp^{(b)}_k$ is the smallest draw-$b$ $p$-value among the candidates ranked
$k$ or later, and $\bar p_c$ is the adjusted $p$-value of candidate $c$ at its
rank. For the family-wise level $\alpha\in(0,1)$ (\texttt{--alpha}),
candidate $c$ is \emph{significant} when $\bar p_c\le\alpha$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Family-wise error]
\label{prop:fwer}
Under $H_0^{\mathrm{blk},1}$ and subset pivotality,
$\Pr(\exists\, c\in I_0:\bar p_c\le\alpha)\le\alpha$.
\end{proposition}

This is the Westfall--Young result: the permutations supply the joint
distribution of the $p$-values, dependence included, so no independence
between candidates is assumed.

\begin{remark}[Why observation and draws share one pooled set]
Scoring each draw only against the other draws gives it denominator $B$ and a
smallest possible value of $1/B$, while the observation reaches $1/(B+1)$. An
observation at its smallest value could then never be matched, and the
adjustment rejected $10$--$16\%$ of the time at $\alpha=0.05$ on exchangeable
counts. Scoring everything among the same $B+1$ values, as in
Definition~\ref{def:p}, restores the nominal level.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}{how many lottery tickets win by luck?}
With 823 candidates that are all pure luck and a $5\%$ threshold each, you
expect $823\times0.05\approx41$ false hits, and the chance of at least one is
$1-0.95^{823}$, indistinguishable from $1$. Without the adjustment, a search
this size \emph{always} finds something.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{three recipes, nine pretend maps}
\small

\section{How many pretend maps are enough?}
\label{sec:budget}

\begin{plain}
With 9 pretend maps the best you can ever say is ``one time in ten''. With a
big list of recipes it gets worse: on almost every pretend map \emph{some}
recipe will look as extreme as it can, so the luckiest-of-all bar is almost
always at the top. You then need so many pretend maps that even the luckiest
recipe rarely hits the top. As a rule of thumb, about twenty pretend maps for
every recipe on the list.
\end{plain}

Every $p$-value is a multiple of $1/(B+1)$, and the smallest possible value,
the \emph{floor}, is $1/(B+1)$. If the $C$ candidates behaved independently, a
fraction $\approx C/(B+1)$ of the draws would have some candidate at its
floor, so
\[
  \min_{c}\bar p_c\ \gtrsim\ \frac{C}{B+1},
  \qquad\text{hence rejecting at level }\alpha\text{ needs }\ B\gtrsim\frac{C}{\alpha}.
\]
Correlated candidates, which near-duplicate recipes are, do better. The
implementation warns when $B+1<20\,C$.

\begin{example}{823 candidates on LSP31891}
With $C=823$ and $B=199$ the bound is $823/200\approx4$: no recipe could ever
be declared significant. With $B=19{,}999$ it is $0.041$, and the run
reported significant motifs at $\bar p_c=0.0136$, below the bound because the
candidates are correlated.
\end{example}

\section{Several samples in one run}
\label{sec:multi}

\begin{plain}
Now we have several maps, one per patient. Each map keeps its own squares, and
stickers are \emph{never} swapped between maps --- a pretend version of patient
1 only ever uses patient 1's stickers. We pick candidate recipes from the grey
squares of all maps together, count each recipe on the white squares of every
map, and add the counts up. Then the same luck machine and the same
luckiest-of-all rule decide which recipes are real. Because every pretend round
shuffles all maps at once, we also get each map's own story for free: for
every recipe we can say on how many of the maps it stands out by itself. A
recipe that is strong in the sum but shows up on only one map is a finding
about that patient, not yet about the disease.
\end{plain}

\begin{definition}[Samples]
\label{def:samples}
With $M$ input files, the samples $s=1,\dots,M$ partition the cells,
$\cells=\bigsqcup_{s=1}^M\cells_s$, each sample with its own coordinate
system. Cell types are matched across samples by name, $\types$ numbering the
union of the names. Neighbourhoods stay within a sample:
\[
  \Nb{i}=\{j\in\cells_s:\lVert x_j-x_i\rVert_2\le r\}\qquad\text{for }i\in\cells_s.
\]
Tiles, the checkerboard and interiority are built per sample in that
sample's coordinates. For sample $s$, $W_{s,1},\dots,W_{s,G_s}$ are its $G_s$
non-empty tiles; $S_{0,s}$ and $S_{1,s}$ are its selection and testing
centres; $A_{0,s}$ and $A_{1,s}$ are its selection and testing halves. The
sets $S_0$, $S_1$, $A_0$ and $A_1$ are the unions of these over samples.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Product null]
\label{def:product}
The multi-sample null states that, conditional on the positions, on
$\lab^{\obs}|_{A_0}$ and on the label multiset of every $W_{s,g}\cap A_{1,s}$,
$\lab^{\obs}$ is uniformly distributed over its images under
\[
  \prod_{s=1}^{M}\ \prod_{g=1}^{G_s}\ \Sym\bigl(W_{s,g}\cap A_{1,s}\bigr).
\]
Labels never move between samples. Under the global null each sample is a
single tile.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Pooled and per-sample statistics]
\label{def:pooled}
The family $\mathcal F=\{v_1,\dots,v_C\}$ is built by
Definition~\ref{def:family} on the selection centres $S_0$ of all samples.
For candidate $c$, sample $s$ and $b=0,\dots,B$ (with $\lab^{(0)}=\lab^{\obs}$),
\[
  N^{(b)}_{c,s}=N\bigl(v_c;\lab^{(b)},S_{1,s}\bigr),\qquad
  N^{(b)}_c=\sum_{s=1}^{M}N^{(b)}_{c,s},
\]
with $N^{\obs}_{c,s}=N^{(0)}_{c,s}$ and $N^{\obs}_c=N^{(0)}_c$. The pooled
count $N^{(b)}_c$ equals $N(v_c;\lab^{(b)},S_1)$, and the family-wise test of
Section~\ref{sec:wy} is applied to it. Each draw permutes all samples
simultaneously, so the same draws also give per-sample evidence: the null
mean $\bar N_{c,s}$ and lift $\lift_{c,s}$ of Definition~\ref{def:effect}
computed from $N^{(b)}_{c,s}$, the per-sample $p$-value
\[
  p_{c,s}=\frac{1+|\{b\in\{1,\dots,B\}:N^{(b)}_{c,s}\ge N^{\obs}_{c,s}\}|}{B+1},
\]
and the replication count $\rep_c=|\{s:p_{c,s}\le\alpha\}|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Pooled validity]
\label{prop:multi}
Under the product null, Propositions~\ref{prop:valid} and~\ref{prop:fwer} hold
for the pooled counts $N_c^{(b)}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Neighbourhoods and shuffles stay inside samples, so Lemmas~\ref{lem:sep}
and~\ref{lem:meas} hold sample by sample, and $\mathcal F$ is a function of
$\lab^{\obs}|_{A_0}$. Given that, $N^{(b)}_c$ is a fixed function of the
testing labels of draw $b$, and the product null makes the observed and
permuted labellings i.i.d.\ as in Remark~\ref{rem:draws}; the pooled counts
are therefore exchangeable and the proofs carry over unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the per-sample numbers are, and are not]
The per-sample $p$-values $p_{c,s}$ are not adjusted for the family and carry
no error guarantee; $\rep_c$ is descriptive. They answer a different
question: \emph{where} does the pooled evidence come from? A large sample can
carry $N^{\obs}_c$ on its own, so a pooled discovery with $\rep_c=1$ should be
read as a strong result in one tissue.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}{two patients}
A recipe $v$ appears 30 times on patient 1's white squares against a null mean
of 10, and 2 times on patient 2's against a null mean of 2. Pooled, it appears
32 times against 12: clearly more than luck, and it may well be significant
after adjustment. But its per-sample $p$-value is tiny for patient 1 and large
for patient 2, so its replication count is 1: the signal is patient 1's. A
recipe with 12 against 6 in both would pool to 24 against 12 --- a similar
lift --- with replication count 2, a stronger claim about the disease.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{checking that samples never mix}
Two samples share every coordinate; one is entirely of one cell type, the
other entirely of a second type. If circles crossed samples, mixtures of the
two types would appear; if shuffles crossed samples, the pretend maps would
dilute the pure recipes and give small $p$-values. The implementation finds
only the two pure recipes, each with $p=1$ under both nulls: no pretend map
can differ from the real one. This is a test in the suite
(\texttt{multi\_sample\_separation}).
\end{example}

\section{What gets reported}
\label{sec:report}

\begin{plain}
From the recipes that survived the lottery rule, we skip copies that are
nearly the same as one already on the list, and skip any that do not come back
in enough separate places on the checking squares. Throwing away winners can
never create a false one, so this does not weaken the guarantee.
\end{plain}

Candidates are taken in the ranking order of Definition~\ref{def:wy}.
Candidate $c$ is skipped when $d(v_c,v_{c'})\le2\rho$ for a candidate $c'$
already reported, or when $D(v_c;\lab^{\obs},S_1)<\kappa$; reporting stops
after \texttt{--max-motifs} candidates.

\begin{proposition}[Post-hoc filtering is safe]
Let $\mathcal R=\{c:\bar p_c\le\alpha\}$ be the significant candidates and
$\mathcal R'\subseteq\mathcal R$ those kept by any rule. Then
$\Pr(\mathcal R'\cap I_0\ne\emptyset)\le\Pr(\mathcal R\cap I_0\ne\emptyset)\le\alpha$.
\end{proposition}

Each reported candidate carries $N^{\obs}_c$, $\bar N_c$, $\lift_c$, $z_c$,
$p_c$, $\bar p_c$ and $D(v_c;\lab^{\obs},S_1)$; with several samples, also
$N^{\obs}_{c,s}$, $\bar N_{c,s}$, $\lift_{c,s}$ and $p_{c,s}$ for every sample
and $\rep_c$.

\section{Algorithm, cost and parameters}
\label{sec:algo}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item Read every sample, drop \texttt{unclassified}, match cell types by name.
  \item Build $\Nb{i}$ within each sample with a hash grid of cell size $r$.
  \item Split each sample into selection, testing and boundary centres.
  \item On all selection centres: classes, frequencies, disjoint support, the
        family $\mathcal F$.
  \item On each sample's testing centres: $N^{\obs}_{c,s}$, summed to $N^{\obs}_c$.
  \item For $b=1,\dots,B$: one within-tile shuffle of every sample's testing
        labels; per sample, compositions, classes, one tree and $C$ queries;
        store $N^{(b)}_c$ in a $C\times B$ table and add $N^{(b)}_{c,s}$ to the
        per-sample running totals.
  \item Pooled-rank $p$-values, step-down adjustment, reporting.
\end{enumerate}

Let $\bar\nu$ be the mean neighbourhood size $|\Nb{i}|$ over testing centres
and $Q_s$ the number of classes among the testing centres of sample $s$ in
one draw. One permutation costs
$O\bigl(\sum_{s=1}^M(|S_{1,s}|\,\bar\nu+Q_s\ln Q_s+C\ln Q_s)\bigr)$; the
adjustment costs $O(CB\ln B)$ time and memory for $O(CB)$ numbers. Pooling
samples enlarges the family, and with it both the budget $B$ and the cost of
each draw.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{``Not luck'' is not ``these types like each other''.} Any
        arrangement finer than the tiles --- including one cell type clumping
        with itself --- beats the block null. The composition filter, or a null
        preserving each type's own clustering, is needed to target
        co-localisation of different types.
  \item \textbf{Tile sizes are part of the question.} $L$ and $\Lambda$ must be
        fixed before looking at the results.
  \item \textbf{Half of each tissue chooses, half checks.} A motif confined to
        one region may be chosen and then not confirmed.
  \item \textbf{Reported vectors are class representatives,} the first count
        vector of each class; only their proportions are meaningful.
  \item \textbf{Pooling is not replication.} The pooled test controls error for
        the pooled counts; replication across samples is reported but not
        adjusted, and a large tissue can carry a pooled result alone.
  \item \textbf{Discreteness} makes the $p$-values conservative rather than
        exactly uniform.
\end{enumerate}
